Evaluating Adaptation for Imbalanced PCAOB Audit-Finding Classification: Accuracy, Efficiency, and Exploratory Lexical Masking

Short title: Domain Adaptation for PCAOB Finding Classification

Summary

We compare adaptation strategies for classifying PCAOB finding narratives into a researcher-defined 12-class mapping. In the prespecified primary comparison on a retrospective 890-finding test, weighted LoRA scored 0.0272 Macro-F1 below weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning, but the unadjusted 95% hierarchical-bootstrap interval crossed zero [−0.0587, 0.0097]; a reliable difference was therefore not established. Descriptively, SEC-BERT full fine-tuning had the highest mean Macro-F1, whereas LoRA reduced trainable parameters and peak memory but not training time. Standard class weighting did not improve low-frequency Macro-F1 or Macro-Recall. Removing 27 post-hoc detected near duplicates left condition ordering unchanged. Matched lexical masking was exploratory and incomplete. The results characterize adaptation under mapping-derived labels; they neither validate the taxonomy as an accounting measure nor support replacement of PCAOB metadata or prospective deployment.

Keywords: audit analytics; PCAOB inspection findings; domain adaptation; parameter-efficient fine-tuning; class imbalance

1. Introduction

1.1 Research Motivation and Problem

Public inspection findings are an important, structured window into audit deficiencies, but their narratives remain analytically demanding. The Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) publishes descriptions of deficiencies and, since April 2025, machine-readable Part I.A data containing both the full description and an audit-area attribute (PCAOB, 2025). That availability changes the appropriate research question. A classifier is not presently needed merely to fill the field in the downloadable file. Instead, narrative classification is scientifically useful as a controlled test of whether accounting language models recover a research taxonomy, how design choices behave under severe class imbalance, and whether apparent accuracy depends on explicit lexical cues.

This distinction matters for accounting information systems. Models that perform well on common classes can still fail on less frequent categories; parameter-efficient techniques may reduce memory without reducing elapsed time; and domain pretraining may or may not justify its additional specialization. An applied evaluation should therefore report not only an aggregate score, but also class-level behavior, seed variability, computational cost, and evidence from lexical masking. It should also distinguish validated results from potential uses. Historical PDF extraction, harmonization across heterogeneous regulatory sources, or coding documents lacking standardized metadata are plausible future contexts, but this study does not test them. Moreover, no independent accounting experts assessed whether the 12 mapped classes form a reliable or useful research construct. Accordingly, the study evaluates models conditional on those labels; it does not validate a new accounting measurement instrument.

1.2 Research Gap and Study Objective

We compare three strategies. Full fine-tuning updates every model parameter for the target task. Domain pretraining means that the encoder was initially trained on financial-domain material before task adaptation; SEC-BERT is compared with general-domain BERT-base. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning updates a much smaller set of parameters; our implementation uses low-rank adaptation (LoRA), which inserts trainable low-rank matrices into selected attention transformations while leaving most base-model weights fixed (Hu et al., 2022). We also compare class-weighted and unweighted loss where prespecified, and original with matched-masked text conditions.

Prior audit-text studies demonstrate the feasibility of supervised classification and information retrieval, but they do not provide the same fixed, report-disjoint comparison of domain-specific full fine-tuning, parameter-efficient adaptation, class imbalance, duplicate sensitivity, and implementation cost for PCAOB finding narratives. The objective is therefore to evaluate adaptation choices and their evidential boundaries under one frozen design, rather than to propose a new algorithm or replace the official PCAOB metadata.

1.3 Research Questions

The following questions reproduce the substance of the Phase 1 protocol frozen before official training. They determine the evidential hierarchy. Later result headings organize the completed analyses for readability; they are presentation themes rather than replacement or post-hoc “frozen” questions.

Prespecified RQ1—Effectiveness, stability, and cost. Under identical report-grouped evaluation, how do sparse, frozen, parameter-efficient, and full fine-tuning strategies compare in classification effectiveness, run-to-run stability, and computational cost? The primary outcome is test Macro-F1; accuracy, balanced accuracy, trainable parameters, peak GPU memory, training time, and inference throughput are secondary outcomes.

Prespecified RQ2—Minority classes and weighting. How do adaptation strategy and class-weighted loss affect high-, medium-, and low-frequency class performance and stability? The prespecified primary RQ2 outcomes are low-frequency Macro-F1 and low-frequency Macro-Recall. Per-class metrics, high- and medium-frequency Macro-F1, and worst-seed Macro-F1 are secondary.

Prespecified RQ3—Domain pretraining and lexical cues. Do domain pretraining and adaptation depth improve performance when explicit audit-area lexical cues are naturally absent or removed under matched training and evaluation? The protocol separated three branches: a naturally cue-absent test subset; matched masked training and evaluation; and original-text training followed by masked-input evaluation. Only the matched-masked branch was completed. Domain-model comparisons are reported as prespecified secondary contrasts, and matched masking is reported as an incomplete exploratory analysis rather than a complete answer to RQ3.

The frozen experiment contains eight conditions and three official seeds, or 24 runs, with Macro-F1 as the primary metric. On the 890-finding retrospective test, the prespecified weighted LoRA–weighted SEC-BERT full-fine-tuning comparison did not establish a reliable difference because its interval crossed zero. Descriptively, unweighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning achieved the highest mean Macro-F1 (0.8950). A post-hoc audit found 27 missed cross-partition near duplicates, but excluding them did not change the condition ordering. Detailed estimates and evidential qualifications are reported in Section 6.

1.4 Contributions of the Study

The contribution is threefold. First, it supplies an accounting-centered comparison of domain-specific full fine-tuning, general-domain full fine-tuning, and LoRA under a fixed report-grouped design. Second, it quantifies an implementation trade-off: LoRA reduced trainable parameters to 1.09% of the full SEC-BERT count and lowered peak GPU memory by about 75%, yet took roughly 46% longer to train than the comparable SEC-BERT full-fine-tuning run. Third, it documents evidential boundaries—class imbalance, prior test access, missed near duplicates, and an incompletely training-derived masking vocabulary—rather than treating a single benchmark score as operational readiness.

These contributions are deliberately bounded. The taxonomy derives from PCAOB audit-area metadata; the test is retrospective; the models are not proposed as replacements for official metadata; and the study does not establish generalization across time, audit firms, or external data sources.

2. Literature Review and Research Positioning

2.1 Audit-Text Classification

The PCAOB inspects registered public accounting firms and publishes portions of firm inspection reports. Part I.A describes deficiencies that PCAOB staff considered sufficiently significant that the firm had not obtained sufficient appropriate audit evidence to support its audit opinion. The downloadable dataset announced in 2025 covers multiple years, provides the complete Part I.A deficiency description, and includes attributes such as audit area, affected audit, and cited auditing standard (PCAOB, 2025). Official metadata remain the authoritative source for uses involving the released dataset.

Our target is different from the official field. The project maps normalized source audit-area values into 12 broader, researcher-defined classes designed for comparative modeling. The taxonomy is a measurement choice, not a professional standard and not a claim about the correct substantive classification of every finding. Because the mapping was not independently reviewed by accounting experts, its construct validity remains unestablished. It is adequate as the fixed target of a comparative model experiment, but it should not be treated as a validated research measure, confused with the original PCAOB label, or used to overrule it.

Textual analysis converts narrative communication into variables or classifications used in research and decision support. Accounting applications increasingly employ contextual language models, but construct validity, reproducibility, and alignment between a text measure and its intended concept remain central (Bochkay et al., 2023). In a regulated setting, implementation evidence includes the computational footprint, failure distribution, and provenance of data transformations, not only mean predictive performance. Recent audit-AI research similarly emphasizes contextual models, human oversight, and explainability rather than autonomous replacement of professional judgment (Bhattacharya & Mickovic, 2024; Gu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2022).

Accounting textual analysis has moved from dictionaries and bag-of-words measures toward contextual models that represent a word in relation to surrounding language (Bochkay et al., 2023). Applications show that contextual learning can improve accounting classification, while also raising questions about construct definition, validation, and interpretation (Bhattacharya & Mickovic, 2024). PCAOB findings have also been coded into study-specific taxonomies to examine economic consequences, demonstrating both the value and the judgment involved in categorizing inspection text (Constance et al., 2025). Our study focuses on classification performance and implementation trade-offs rather than economic outcomes.

2.2 Domain-Specific Language Models

BERT is a bidirectional Transformer encoder pretrained on general text and then adapted to downstream tasks (Devlin et al., 2019). Domain-pretrained models continue this logic using specialized corpora. SEC-BERT was pretrained on SEC filings and released in work on financial numeric entity recognition (Loukas et al., 2022). Domain vocabulary and pretraining may help with financial language, but performance gains remain task- and dataset-dependent; this motivates a controlled comparison against a version-pinned BERT-base.

2.3 Full Fine-Tuning and Parameter-Efficient Adaptation

Full fine-tuning updates all weights. LoRA instead represents task-specific weight changes through lower-rank matrices, greatly reducing the number of trainable parameters (Hu et al., 2022). Parameter efficiency does not automatically imply shorter wall-clock training or faster inference: adapter operations, checkpointing, small physical batches, and framework implementations can alter throughput. Hence we measure parameters, time, peak memory, and inference rate rather than inferring them from method labels.

2.4 Class Imbalance in Accounting-Text Classification

Class imbalance means that some labels occur far more often than others. Standard training loss can therefore be dominated by common classes. A class-weighted loss assigns greater penalty to errors in rarer training classes, but it need not improve Macro-F1 in every setting (He & Garcia, 2009). Lexical cues introduce a related concern. Models can learn shortcuts—features correlated with labels in the benchmark but not necessarily stable across contexts (Geirhos et al., 2020). Our masking analysis asks whether performance persists when audit-area phrases are removed, but it is exploratory because masked models were separately trained and the vocabulary was globally constructed.

2.5 Positioning, Evaluation Dependence, and Incremental Contribution

Evaluation design must reflect dependence within data. Findings from the same inspection report can share wording, firm context, and templates. Random row splitting could place closely related findings in both training and evaluation. We therefore group by report_id, following the broader principle that validation blocks should match the dependence structure (Roberts et al., 2017). The subsequent near-duplicate audit shows that group separation alone does not eliminate repeated language across different reports.

More directly related audit-classification studies clarify the incremental contribution. Hedback (2025) uses supervised learning to identify going-concern-modified audit reports and audit-report pages, where the target is an observable reporting outcome and the intended contribution includes information retrieval. Vaishampayan et al. (2023) classify sentences in Indian audit reports into 21 checklist-derived, potentially multilabel coverage categories using weak supervision, BERT, rules, active learning, and large-language-model review; their test set comprises ten manually labeled audit reports. The present study instead starts from PCAOB inspection findings, deterministically aggregates an already available audit-area field into 12 single-label research categories, groups partitions by inspection report, and compares domain-pretrained full adaptation, LoRA, general-domain adaptation, imbalance treatment, computational cost, and duplicate sensitivity. Its incremental value is therefore a controlled adaptation and data-integrity comparison, not discovery of an otherwise unavailable reporting outcome or validation of a new accounting construct.

3. Data and Taxonomy Construction

3.1 Unit of Analysis, Taxonomy, and Split

The unit of analysis is a PCAOB finding narrative. Source labels were normalized and deterministically mapped to 12 coarse classes (Appendix A). The working dataset contains 6,607 findings. A fixed seeded procedure assigned entire inspection reports—not individual rows—to training, validation, calibration, and test. Thus a report_id appears in only one partition. Validation selected learning rates, adapters, epochs, and checkpoints; the calibration partition was reserved and not loaded during the 24 Phase 5 training runs. The Phase 6 test remained retrospective because an older Router project had already accessed it.

3.3 Exclusions, Conflicts, and Near Duplicates

The original pipeline excluded exact or high-similarity cross-partition text according to project rules and marked retained evaluation rows clean_eval=true. The later audit found the nearest-neighbor implementation was incomplete. Using the intended character-TF-IDF cosine similarity threshold of 0.90 over all cross-partition pairs found missed matches. The test diagnostic excludes the 27 affected rows but leaves training and model fitting unchanged. Twenty-five of the 27 test findings had the same coarse class as their closest earlier match, so their inclusion could make evaluation easier, although the realized effect is assessed empirically in Section 6.5.

An earlier fine-label conflict rule removed 245 rows. After collapsing to the current 12 classes, 159 would no longer be conflicting. The retained data also contain six groups (12 rows) of identical normalized text assigned different coarse labels in training or validation. These facts are data-quality and sample-definition limitations; we do not silently reconstruct the dataset after seeing outcomes.

4. Experimental Design

4.1 Evaluated Models and Adaptation Methods

SEC-BERT-base is a BERT-family encoder pretrained on SEC filings (Loukas et al., 2022). Full fine-tuning updates the encoder and the 12-class classification head. General-domain BERT-base-uncased provides a control with similar architecture but different pretraining. Version revisions were pinned before official runs (Appendix B). LoRA leaves the base SEC-BERT weights fixed and learns low-rank changes to selected attention projections plus the classification head.

Three deterministic comparators anchor model performance: term frequency–inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) features with a linear classifier, a frozen MiniLM sentence encoder with a linear head, and frozen SEC-BERT representations with a linear head.

4.2 Adapter and Learning-Rate Selection

A validation-only, single-seed pilot compared four LoRA structures at a common learning rate. QKVO–R16 achieved validation Macro-F1 0.8739 and was selected; it had 1,188,876 trainable parameters, rank 16, scaling parameter 32, and dropout 0.05. This development budget favored LoRA by giving it an extra structural search not given to the full-fine-tuning controls, yet the search used only one seed and all candidates reached the six-epoch cap. Both facts temper the comparison.

Learning-rate pilots used seed 20261113, original text, weighted loss, and clean validation only. Selected rates were 2×10−4 for LoRA, 2×10−5 for SEC-BERT full fine-tuning, and 3×10−5 for BERT-base full fine-tuning. LoRA and BERT-base selected the upper boundary of their respective grids, so the optimum may lie outside the searched range.

4.3 Frozen Experimental Matrix

Unweighted cross-entropy treats every training row equally. Weighted loss calculates each training-only class weight as N/(12nk), where N is the training-set size and nk is the count for class k; its sample-frequency-weighted mean is one. This increases the contribution of rare classes. Weighted and unweighted original-text runs were frozen for SEC-BERT LoRA and SEC-BERT full fine-tuning; the general-domain and masked branches use weighted loss only. The resulting design is eight conditions, not the full 12-condition Cartesian product.

For matched masking, the same audit-area phrase removal was applied to training and evaluation text and each masked model was trained separately. The row-level transform did not read that row’s true label. However, the 245-phrase vocabulary was generated from a global taxonomy mapping: 59 phrases were absent from the train-derived mapping and those phrases matched 109 originally retained test narratives. Therefore the preprocessing is not fully training-derived or leakage-free, and original-versus-masked differences are not a pure within-model causal effect.

4.4 Training and Checkpoint-Selection Procedures

Official seeds were 20261114, 20261115, and 20261116. All checkpoints were chosen by highest validation Macro-F1, with earlier epoch favored under the stated tie rule. The official Phase 5 trainer loaded training and validation partitions only; neither the retrospective test nor the reserved calibration partition was used for parameter fitting or checkpoint selection.

5. Evaluation Design and Statistical Analysis

For class k, F1 is the harmonic mean of precision (the share of predictions for class k that are correct) and recall (the share of true class-k findings recovered). Macro-F1 is the arithmetic mean across the 12 class F1 values:

Macro-F1 = (1/12) Σk=112 F1k.

The prespecified primary estimand is the difference in mean test Macro-F1 between weighted SEC-BERT LoRA and weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning across the three paired official seeds, calculated as LoRA minus full fine-tuning. We report the mean, sample standard deviation, and worst seed across the three official runs. Prespecified pairwise differences use 5,000 hierarchical bootstrap replicates that resample inspection reports and incorporate seed variation. This report-cluster bootstrap retains the report as the sampling unit rather than pretending findings within a report are independent. The 95% intervals are percentile intervals and are not adjusted for the five comparisons. With only three seeds, seed standard deviations are descriptive. Run-level results and report-cluster intervals are provided in Supplementary Table S1; seed-by-tier F1 and Recall are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Efficiency measures use the recorded official runs: trainable and total parameters, elapsed training minutes, maximum allocated GPU memory, selected epoch, and inference examples per second. They describe this software/hardware implementation, not universal method properties.

6.5 Post-Hoc Near-Duplicate Sensitivity Analysis

Removing 27 test findings that had an exhaustive cross-partition similarity match reduced mean Macro-F1 by 0.0014 to 0.0041. The two highest original-text conditions remained SEC-BERT full fine-tuning, followed by LoRA and BERT-base in the same condition-level order. Validation was also affected: 44 of 927 originally clean validation findings had missed earlier near duplicates. Because adapter, learning-rate, and checkpoint selection used that validation set, test-only exclusion cannot fully repair selection bias. Existing validation predictions can be filtered diagnostically, but a genuinely clean confirmatory study would require rebuilding partitions and repeating selection before accessing a new test.

Label conflict and sample-definition sensitivity.

The integrity audit found that the original fine-grained conflict exclusion was conservative relative to the final coarse taxonomy and that six retained normalized-text groups have conflicting coarse labels. We report these counts as measurement uncertainty. Reintroducing or relabeling observations after outcome inspection would create a new design; it is therefore left for a future versioned dataset rather than mixed into the frozen results.

6.6 Exploratory Lexical Masking

Matched masking reduced weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning from 0.8929 to 0.6489, LoRA from 0.8656 to 0.6171, and BERT-base from 0.8590 to 0.6169 on the original retrospective test. These are large descriptive differences, consistent with strong reliance on explicit audit-area phrases and related surface cues. They are not causal estimates of how the same fitted model would behave after removing words: each masked condition was trained on masked text, the masking vocabulary incorporated global mapping information, and the natural cue-absent and original-trained-to-masked-input branches were not run.

7. Discussion

7.1 RQ1: Adaptation Effectiveness, Stability, and Cost

The evidence answers the prespecified primary question in qualified terms. Weighted LoRA scored below weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning on average, but the primary hierarchical-bootstrap interval crossed zero; a reliable primary-comparison difference was not established. Full fine-tuning's highest condition means are therefore descriptive.

LoRA delivered its intended parameter and memory reductions but not the highest mean or shortest time. This result is valuable for intelligent-systems implementation because it separates several notions often collapsed into “efficiency.” A team constrained by GPU memory or adapter storage may rationally accept the observed descriptive performance gap. A team with a single modern GPU and a small dataset may prefer full fine-tuning because it was faster here, while recognizing that the primary comparison did not establish a reliable accuracy difference. Hardware, framework, batch construction, and gradient checkpointing can change this balance; the tables make the local conditions auditable.

7.2 RQ2: Minority-Class Performance and Weighting

Class weighting did not improve the mean for either SEC-BERT strategy. Low-frequency classes remained weaker, and the unweighted models had slightly higher values for both prespecified low-tier outcomes, Macro-F1 and Macro-Recall. Possible explanations include noisy rare labels, already separable lexical markers, overcompensation by inverse-frequency weights, and high variance from small supports. The result argues against assuming that this standard weighting formula solves imbalance. It does not show that all reweighting, resampling, focal loss, or thresholding approaches would fail.

7.3 RQ3: Domain Pretraining and Lexical Cues

In the prespecified secondary domain-model comparison, weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning exceeded weighted BERT-base by 0.0339 Macro-F1. That observation is consistent with task-relevant domain representations, but it is not a multiplicity-adjusted or causal estimate of pretraining-domain effects: model vocabularies, pretraining details, and downstream optimization coexist, the test was historically exposed, and the study covers one derived taxonomy.

The masking decline and the strength of TF-IDF suggest that lexical surface cues are central to this benchmark. For a reconstruction task, such cues may be legitimate evidence: audit-area terminology is part of the narrative. The concern is transfer. If the vocabulary or drafting conventions change, a system dependent on explicit phrases may generalize poorly. Because the implemented masking analysis has known leakage and design limitations, the proper conclusion is diagnostic: cue dependence deserves prospective testing, not that the models have been proven to rely on a specific causal shortcut.

7.4 Data Integrity and Evidential Boundaries

Finally, the post-hoc integrity findings demonstrate why reproducibility is more than releasing a score. The group split correctly prevented the same report from crossing partitions, yet repeated text across reports remained. The small realized change after excluding 27 test rows is reassuring about condition ordering, but it does not retroactively make the original test untouched, nor does it remove the 44 affected validation rows from model selection. Transparent reporting is therefore more defensible than a silent rerun on a modified test.

8. Implications for Accounting Research and Practice

8.1 Implications for Accounting Research

Researchers constructing text-based variables from inspection narratives should define the construct before selecting the model. The 12 classes in this study are a research aggregation, not a direct substitute for PCAOB metadata. Report-grouped splitting should be a minimum safeguard when multiple findings share a report, and exhaustive duplicate checks should occur before model selection. Macro-F1 and class supports should accompany aggregate accuracy whenever the resulting variable is used in downstream inference; otherwise, minority-category measurement error may be hidden.

The secondary positive SEC-BERT–BERT gap suggests that domain-pretrained encoders merit inclusion in accounting benchmark sets, but it should not be promoted above the inconclusive primary comparison or interpreted as a confirmed general advantage. At the same time, the TF-IDF baseline and masking results show that simple lexical models remain informative diagnostic comparators. A credible accounting NLP study benefits from both: contextual models quantify potential gains, while transparent baselines reveal how much signal can be captured without deep adaptation.

8.2 Implications for Intelligent-Systems Design

System choice should follow the binding resource. LoRA minimized trainable state and memory, whereas full fine-tuning minimized elapsed time and improved mean performance on this hardware. Practitioners should benchmark these dimensions locally. Any future decision-support use should preserve the original narrative and official metadata, log the model/version, expose class uncertainty, and route ambiguous or out-of-scope cases to human review. These are design recommendations, not evidence that the present models are calibrated or ready for production.

8.3 What Is and Is Not Practically Established

The study establishes comparative behavior on a fixed, report-disjoint, retrospective PCAOB-derived dataset. It does not validate deployment, future-report performance, cross-source interoperability, or replacement of the existing PCAOB field; any use beyond methodological benchmarking requires new external evidence.

9. Limitations and Future Research

9.1 Limitations Affecting Numerical Estimates

First, the test was accessed during an earlier Router study and cannot be called untouched. Second, the original near-duplicate routine missed 27 of 890 clean-test rows and 44 of 927 clean-validation rows. The 863-row exclusion is post-hoc and cannot remove validation-stage influence. Third, exact-text label conflicts and a coarse-taxonomy mismatch in earlier exclusions affect sample composition. Fourth, there are only three official seeds. Fifth, the smallest test class has eight examples, with other low-frequency classes also small; class estimates and Macro-F1 are correspondingly variable. Sixth, the 12-class mapping received no independent expert review, blind recoding, or inter-rater reliability assessment, so predictive agreement with its labels does not establish construct validity. The reported bootstrap intervals are not multiplicity-adjusted.

9.2 Limitations Affecting Causal Interpretation

The original and masked conditions are separately trained models, so their difference is not a within-model causal effect of removing words. The masking vocabulary was generated from a global mapping; 59 of 245 phrases were absent from the train-derived mapping and affected 109 original clean-test rows. Only the matched-masked branch of the three-part lexical-cue plan was completed. Likewise, the SEC-BERT versus BERT comparison does not vary only pretraining domain, and class weighting was evaluated with one formula. These analyses support comparisons among implemented conditions, not universal causal claims about domain pretraining, cues, or imbalance remedies.

9.3 Limitations Affecting External Validity and Scope

The official PCAOB downloadable data already contain audit-area information; the study evaluates reconstruction from narratives rather than a currently necessary metadata-filling operation. The taxonomy is researcher-defined, and the evidence comes from one PCAOB-derived dataset. We did not test prospective reports, time-based holdouts, unseen audit firms, historical PDFs as a distinct source, or other regulators. Thus cross-time, cross-firm, and cross-document-source generalization are unestablished. Adapter selection and learning-rate development used limited single-seed budgets; LoRA received an additional structural pilot, and two selected learning rates lay at grid boundaries. Only one LoRA design, two base encoders, and a limited set of adaptation and imbalance methods were tested. No deployment, auditor-time saving, or replacement of professional judgment is claimed.

9.4 Future Research

A stronger next study should freeze a corrected, fully training-derived preprocessing and duplicate-control pipeline before evaluating it once on new reports separated by time, audit firm, and document source. Independent accounting experts could blindly review a stratified label sample to assess the construct validity of the 12-class aggregation. Future work could also complete the naturally cue-absent and original-trained-to-masked-input branches, and compare additional parameter-efficient adaptation and imbalance remedies under the same report-grouped design.

10. Conclusion

In this retrospective PCAOB finding-classification experiment, the prespecified primary comparison did not establish a reliable difference between weighted LoRA and weighted SEC-BERT full fine-tuning. Descriptively, SEC-BERT full fine-tuning produced the highest mean Macro-F1, while a secondary comparison showed a positive observed gap between weighted SEC-BERT and weighted general-domain BERT. LoRA sharply reduced trainable parameters and GPU memory but was slower and had lower mean performance in the recorded implementation. Standard class weighting did not improve low-frequency Macro-F1 or Macro-Recall, and the completed matched-masking branch produced large declines consistent with substantial lexical-cue dependence. Excluding 27 post-hoc detected test near duplicates modestly reduced scores without changing condition ordering.

These findings support an empirical, implementation-focused contribution for accounting intelligent systems: adaptation choices should be evaluated jointly on minority-class behavior, computational constraints, and data provenance. They do not justify replacing PCAOB metadata or claiming prospective generalization.
